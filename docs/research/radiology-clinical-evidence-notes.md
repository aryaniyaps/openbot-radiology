# Radiology AI: clinical evidence and direct frontier model experiments

Research date: 2026-10-01. This is an original synthesis of primary research, not a systematic review. Firecrawl paper search, related-paper expansion, metadata inspection and in-body verification were used first; publishers, PMC and Firecrawl's PubMed provider supplied primary text where indexed full text was absent. Latest findings should be interpreted against the models, inputs, endpoints and populations actually tested.

## What worked with direct frontier models

### A bounded CT diagnostic question with clinical context

[Emergency abdominal CT comparison, Tomography 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2379-139X/11/10/108) evaluated **45 retrospectively selected cases**, with **4–7 representative JPEG CT slices per case, 243 images total**, against six radiology residents. GPT-4o returned the correct single diagnosis in **37/45 (82.22%)**, versus resident scores of **34–40/45 (75.56–88.89%)**. Readers and GPT received clinical history, examination and laboratory information. Two radiologists chose and windowed slices that highlighted the emergency abnormality. The absence of a statistically significant difference in 45 cases does not demonstrate equivalence.

Crucially, the authors ran a separate image-only, open-ended findings prompt and reported **75% hallucination**: the fraction of listed findings lacking a correlate on the supplied slices. That is not the fraction of patients with wrong final diagnoses, and is not the same endpoint as 82% single-diagnosis accuracy. This study supports testing structured clinical-context questions on expertly selected images; it does not establish autonomous whole-volume interpretation.

### Diagnosis from existing human-written imaging findings

[Focal liver lesions, Liver International 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/liv.70115) studied **228 patients with 228 single lesions** and histopathology after contrast CT/MRI. GPT-4o received clinical context and the **findings** section, while the **impression** section was used for the human-report comparator. Two-step GPT-4o reached **78.9%**, versus single-step **68.0%**, Gemini **73.2%**, real-world reports **80.0%**, junior radiologists **78.9–82.0%**, and middle-level radiologists **84.6–85.5%**. Adding GPT did not improve any radiologist's diagnostic accuracy. This supports structured reasoning over human-extracted imaging findings; it does not demonstrate reading CT/MRI pixels. The abstract establishes findings/impression separation, but the paywalled body and inaccessible supplement prevent independent verification of the exact two-step prompts or exclusion of diagnostic language within findings.

### Obvious pneumothorax, with major selection dependence

[Spontaneous pneumothorax, BMC Pulmonary Medicine 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12890-025-04041-w) tested GPT-4o on **220 standing PA radiographs: 110 positive, 110 negative**. Image-only binary responses produced **78/110 sensitivity (70.9%)** and **106/110 specificity (96.4%)**. The authors report accuracy 83.7%; the displayed counts imply 184/220, approximately 83.6%, so the rounded paper figure should not be given undue precision. Of the positive cases, **80 were large and 30 small**. Small-pneumothorax AUC was **0.439**, versus **0.894** for large lesions. Postoperative, trauma and ICU cases were excluded. Four surgeons agreed on every reference classification; no radiologist-report comparator was supplied.

[Positive-only pneumothorax cohort, PLOS One 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12431401/) evaluated **172 CT-confirmed positive cases**, including **148 over age 12 and 24 children**, using three repeats with symptoms and textbook priming. The abstract reports adult performance **69.6% GPT-4o, 64.9% Claude 3.5, 57.4% Gemini 2.0**, and pediatric **20.8%, 12.5%, 20.8%**. GPT-4o was better on large than small adult pneumothoraces (**81.6% versus 42.2%**). The manuscript inconsistently names its three-repeat accuracy categories between sections; those labels should not be equated with conventional per-read sensitivity. With no negative cases, specificity and real-world predictive values cannot be established.

[Clinical context and pneumothorax, BMC Pulmonary Medicine 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12890-026-04151-z) reports GPT-5.1 sensitivity **30/30 (100%)**, specificity **91.4%**, and Gemini 2.0 Advanced sensitivity **70%**, specificity **92.9%**, in **240 retrospectively selected thoracic-surgery patients**. This apparent positive has a serious interpretive limitation: the methods say all available data were shared, while listing diagnoses, surgical status and length of stay among collected data. The article does not establish that these outcome-related fields were withheld from input. Therefore, this is not clean evidence of image-only diagnostic sensitivity. The study also found poor localization and intervention decisions. Its laterality denominators are difficult to reconcile with only 30 positives, so laterality percentages should be attributed as reported, not treated as a clean conditional side-identification endpoint.

### Report proofreading is a more credible direct GPT use case

[Radiology 2024 proofreading comparison](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38625012/) used **200 reports**, with **150 deliberately inserted errors in 100 reports**. GPT-4 detected **124/150 (82.7%, 95% CI 75.8–87.9%)**, broadly similar to average readers, though one senior radiologist detected **142/150** and outperformed GPT. Processing time **3.5 seconds versus 25.1 seconds** for the fastest human is an execution-time comparison, not demonstrated total workflow savings including verification. These are text reports, not image diagnosis.

[Radiology 2025 large head-CT proofreading validation](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/10.1148/radiol.240701) used **10,300 reports**: an enriched development/test component and **10,000 unaltered reports**. GPT-4 sensitivities were **84% interpretive and 89% factual errors**, with faster review than humans. The full-text Experiment 2 confirms that GPT-4 flagged **1,792 of the 10,000 presumed-error-free reports**, containing **96 true errors**, yielding **PPV 0.054**. Enriched error-detection performance does not justify automatic edits in ordinary low-error-prevalence reports.

[Real-world abdominal CT report revisions, Abdominal Radiology 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40576664/) tested zero-shot GPT-4 on **600 finalized reports**. It suggested revisions in **91%**, predominantly grammar; radiologists accepted **23%** of suggested revisions. Most had no or low clinical impact; **8% were rated potentially harmful**, including **two potentially severe cases**. Review suggestions are a practical research direction; automatic rewriting is unsupported.

Published diagnostic quizzes and exams show reasoning improvements but test another endpoint. [2024 Japanese board-exam comparison, Academic Radiology 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41318296/) found **Gemini 2.5 Pro Preview 76/100, o3 75/100**, versus a human mean **72.9%**, on 100 multiple-choice questions, 96 image-based. Gemini accuracy on the image subset improved with images (**75.0% versus 63.5%**), showing actual visual contribution. [Residency examination comparison, Academic Radiology 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41224539/) used **282 questions**, 191 text-only and 91 image-based: Gemini 2.5 Pro overall **83.0%**, GPT-5 **82.3%**, residents **78.2%**; residents still led image questions (**80.4% versus GPT-5 73.6%**). Multiple-choice options, selected teaching images and public questions limit hospital applicability and permit contamination concerns.

## Prospective hospital successes: specialized systems and workflows

These demonstrate that radiology AI can work clinically, but they are not evidence that an off-the-shelf GPT/Gemini/Claude can substitute for the tested systems.

## Contrarian clinical evidence

[LungIMPACT randomized trial, Nature Medicine March 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04253-5) analyzed **93,326 CXRs from 86,945 patients at five NHS trusts**. AI prioritization did **not** shorten CXR-to-CT time: geometric-mean ratio **0.97 (95% CI 0.93–1.02)**. Among **558 lung cancers** (269 prioritized, 289 control), diagnosis time was **44 versus 46 median days**, ratio **0.98 (0.83–1.16)**. Positive image detection does not necessarily fix downstream scanner capacity, referrals or care coordination.

[Normal CXR silent trial, Radiology: AI 2026](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42017801) evaluated **63,083 adult CXR** across five sites. AI called **12,422 normal**, with abnormality sensitivity **97%**, specificity **35%**, and NPV **94%**. After excluding **412 NLP reference-label errors**, reviewers identified **31 clinically significant missed findings**, estimated **0.05% of all studies**. That all-study rate should not be confused with risk conditional on being auto-cleared: 31/12,422 is approximately **0.25%** before adjustment for other denominator definitions. This was a silent trial, not proof of safe autonomous reporting.

[behold.ai autonomous-normal CXR preprint 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.16115) selected **930/4,654** studies as high-confidence normal, with **36/930 abnormal discrepancies**, none deemed clinically significant during the study. Site thresholds were retrospectively adjusted to 20% clearance; **99.3% were independently audited within 24h**. This is a narrow selective-autonomy example supported by auditing, a six-week window and vendor-affiliated authors—not elimination of clinical oversight.

[Annalise pneumothorax retrospective validation, JAMA Network Open 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9856508/) used **985 CXR**, 435 PTX and 550 non-PTX: sensitivity **94.3% (95% CI 92.0–96.3%)**, specificity **92.0% (89.6–94.2%)**. This is a strong lesion detector, trained on >750,000 labeled CXR, but case enrichment, 12 inference exclusions and retrospective consensus labels limit prospective safety inference. Small PTX sensitivity was lower (**86.6%**) than ≥2cm lesions (**98.9%**).

## Implications for our next study

1. Test **direct frontier clinical-context reasoning**, **image-only lesion finding**, **localization**, and **text-report checking** as separate endpoints. An impressive final diagnosis can coexist with fabricated findings.
2. Preserve full-resolution source inputs and use targeted windows, appropriate sequences and zooms. Representative CT slices require an image selection mechanism; human-selected study images are privileged inputs compared with a general automated montage.
3. Compare Gemini/GPT/Claude under held-out prompt variants and score sensitivity plus specificity per finding. Do not use high AUC, confidence, valid JSON, overall accuracy on mostly normal cases or multiple-choice performance as deployment proxies.
4. Use existing doctors' reports for feasible offline labels, explicitly separate uncertain/report-image conflict cases, and mark findings whose visibility cannot be established from supplied images. This is compatible with no new live-doctor review, but yields evidence for the defined offline endpoint rather than proof of patient-care safety.
5. A sensible bounded experiment is structured single-diagnosis CT with clinical context plus separately audited visual evidence, followed by an error checker that cannot silently edit the final report. Specialized lesion detectors and domain-trained VLM drafts provide successful comparator architectures, not evidence for an untested direct-frontier replacement.

## Collection limitations

Several Firecrawl research-read calls returned no indexed passages; the new SIIM paper PMCID was not yet accepted by the read endpoint. Primary publisher/PMC full text was used instead. Some PubMed/PMC pages presented CAPTCHA and some Firecrawl calls hit the shared per-minute limit; Europe PMC and the typed PubMed article provider supplied the same primary abstracts. Proofreading and exam findings above are abstract-verified when publisher full text was unavailable. The Cureus full text was read via web fallback after Firecrawl retrieval failed. No vendor marketing was used as independent clinical evidence.
